## Session Information

This hands-on session introduces metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs): how they are obtained from an assembly, and how to check their quality before using them. You'll assess the quality and taxonomy of the bins of the coffee fermentation dataset, then search the bins and the assembly for antimicrobial resistance genes.

## What are MAGs?

Most microbes cannot be cultured in the lab. Metagenomics sequences the DNA of the whole community at once, but this gives a mix of reads from all the organisms. How do we recover individual genomes from this mix?

**Metagenome-Assembled Genomes (MAGs)** are draft genomes reconstructed from metagenomic data, without culturing. The reads are first assembled into longer sequences (contigs), then the contigs that likely come from the same organism are grouped together (binning). Each group of contigs is a **bin**; a good quality bin is a MAG.

**Why generate MAGs?** Read-based profiling tells us who is there and what the community can do. MAGs link functions to organisms: which organism carries which genes. They also give access to organisms that are not in reference databases, including new species.

**The process:**
1. **Assembly:** reads are assembled into contigs (previous session)
2. **Binning:** contigs are grouped into bins
3. **Quality assessment:** completeness and contamination of each bin
4. **Taxonomy:** what organism each MAG represents
5. **Dereplication:** removing redundant MAGs recovered from several samples

## Binning: grouping contigs into genomes

We don't know which organism each contig comes from. Binning tools group contigs using two signals.

**1. Composition.** Each genome has its own sequence "signature": for example, the frequency of short words of 4 nucleotides (tetranucleotides) is fairly constant within a genome, but differs between genomes. Contigs with similar composition are likely from the same genome.

**2. Coverage.** All contigs of a genome come from the same organism, so they should be covered by a similar number of reads. With several samples, the contigs of one organism should also go up and down together across samples.

### When binning fails

Binning works best for abundant organisms that are well separated from their neighbours. It struggles with:
- closely related strains, which have nearly the same composition
- low-abundance organisms, which are poorly assembled
- short contigs, which carry little signal
- mobile elements (plasmids, phages), whose coverage and composition can differ from their host's chromosome

### SemiBin2

The bins of this session were obtained with **SemiBin2**, which uses deep learning and pre-trained models for different environments. Other widely used binning tools include MetaBAT2, CONCOCT and MaxBin2.

## Checking the quality of MAGs

Not all bins are good genomes: some are fragments of a genome, others mix several organisms. Quality assessment is needed before using MAGs.

**Completeness** estimates how much of the genome is present. **Contamination** estimates how much DNA from other genomes was binned with it. Both are estimated from genes expected in every genome.

The community standards for MAG quality (MIMAG, Bowers et al. 2017) define high, medium and low quality MAGs from these two values, and from the presence of rRNA and tRNA genes for high quality MAGs.

In this session:
- **Tiara** tells us whether the contigs of a bin are bacterial, archaeal or eukaryotic. The coffee fermentation also contains yeasts and plant material.
- **CheckM2** estimates completeness and contamination. It was trained on bacteria and archaea: its results are not meaningful for eukaryotic bins.
- **GTDB-Tk** assigns a taxonomy to bacterial and archaeal MAGs.
- **dRep** groups very similar MAGs recovered from different samples, and keeps the best one of each group.
