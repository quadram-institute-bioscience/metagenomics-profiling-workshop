## Why Functional Profiling?

Taxonomic profiling tells us *who* is present in a microbial community, but not what they can *do*. Functional profiling identifies and quantifies the genes and metabolic pathways present in a sample: it describes the **metabolic potential** of the community.

**Key concept: functional redundancy.** Different species can carry the same functions. A change in taxonomic composition does not always mean a change in function, and the same function can be carried by different species at different times.

**Potential, not activity.** Metagenomics sequences DNA: it tells us which genes are present, not which genes are expressed. Measuring activity requires other approaches, such as metatranscriptomics or metabolomics.

## Approaches to Functional Profiling

Two main strategies exist for functional profiling, each with distinct trade-offs.

| Aspect | Gene Catalogue | Read-Based (HUMAnN3) |
|--------|---------------|---------------------|
| **Workflow** | Assemble, predict genes, annotate, then map reads back | Map reads directly to databases of known genes |
| **Assembly needed?** | Yes | No |
| **Novel functions** | Can discover | Limited to the database |
| **Computing time** | Long | Shorter |
| **Best for** | Discovery, poorly characterized environments | Comparative studies, well-characterized environments |

**Which to choose?** Read-based profiling is fast and standardized, and works well to compare samples. Gene catalogues are used for discovery in poorly characterized environments. Many projects use both approaches, as they are complementary.

**In this session**, we use read-based approaches: HUMAnN3 for the whole community, and KMA for targeted gene searches. Assembly is covered in the next sessions.

## HUMAnN3 Overview

HUMAnN3 uses a tiered search strategy to balance speed and sensitivity.

**Step 1: Taxonomic profiling** - Runs MetaPhlAn4 to identify the species present  
**Step 2: Nucleotide search** - Maps reads to the pangenomes of the detected species only (ChocoPhlAn database)  
**Step 3: Translated search** - Reads that did not map are translated and searched against UniRef90 proteins  
**Step 4: Quantification** - Counts reads per gene family and maps gene families to metabolic pathways (MetaCyc)

**Why this works:** searching only the pangenomes of the species present (step 2) makes it fast; the translated search (step 3) catches genes from species that are not in the pangenome database.

### Three main outputs

- **Gene families** (`genefamilies.tsv`): abundance of each gene family (UniRef90 IDs), in RPK (reads per kilobase)
- **Pathway abundance** (`pathabundance.tsv`): abundance of each MetaCyc pathway
- **Pathway coverage** (`pathcoverage.tsv`): fraction of the steps of each pathway that are detected (0 to 1)

### Stratified outputs

HUMAnN3 shows which species contribute to each function:
- **Unstratified** lines (no `|`): community-total abundance
- **Stratified** lines (with `|`): the contribution of each species, for example `FERMENTATION-PWY: mixed acid fermentation|g__Genus.s__Genus_species`

## Targeted Gene Searches

HUMAnN3 gives an overview of all functions. Some questions are about **specific genes**, and use curated databases built for these genes:

| Gene class | Example databases | Example tools |
|---|---|---|
| Antimicrobial resistance genes | ResFinder, CARD | KMA (reads), ABRicate and AMRFinderPlus (contigs) |
| Virulence genes | VFDB | KMA (reads), ABRicate (contigs) |
| Carbohydrate-active enzymes | CAZy | dbCAN |
| Secondary metabolites | | antiSMASH |

In the self-paced exercise, we use **KMA** to map reads directly against ResFinder (resistance genes) and VFDB (virulence genes).

**As with taxonomic profiling:** a gene can only be found if it is in the database, the results depend on the thresholds you choose, and finding a gene does not mean it is expressed.
